RES: Accelerating Diffusion Language Models
with Rolling Entropy Sampling

1Purdue University,2Google DeepMind,*Work done while at Google DeepMind.
A diffusion language sampler makes two choices for every position in its denoising window: where
the next token comes from, and when the position is committed to the KV cache. These choices give
rise to two extremes: committing tokens on a per-position basis, or committing them at a block level.
Using the entropy-bound sampler in DiffusionGemma, we study the tradeoffs between the two. We
simulate per-position commitment by freezing each token upon acceptance, removing the chance for
later revision: macro accuracy falls from 73% to 20% as the entropy bound is relaxed, while the unfrozen
variants stay near 70%. Block-level commitment results in redundant computation, as tokens converge
in a largely left-to-right order and, on average, more than half of the slots are spent on already-final
tokens by twenty denoising steps. We introduce the Rolling Entropy Sampler (RES), which waits for
a sufficiently long contiguous accepted prefix, allowing accepted positions farther ahead to remain
available for revision without waiting for the entire window. Across seven benchmarks, RES raises tokens
per forward pass by 32.5% at an average cost of 1.9 points of accuracy, and extends the speed-quality
frontier beyond what relaxing the entropy bound alone can reach.
1. Introduction
Autoregressive language models train on all tokens in parallel, yet must generate them one at a
time. Diffusion language models remove this limit by denoising many tokens in each forward pass
(DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026; Nie et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025). One promising area for research
is diffusion language model samplers, as many advances in the continuous domain have been driven
by improved sampler performance (Song et al., 2020). The same is true for the language domain,
where sampling order has been shown to have a dramatic effect on task performance (Ben-Hamu
et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025b, 2026a).
Any diffusion language sampler makes two choices for each position in its denoising window. The
first iswherethe next token comes from: the model’s prediction, the token already at that position, or
fresh noise. This defines when a position within a denoising window has converged. The second is
whenthe position is committed to the KV cache, after which it can no longer change. These design
choices give rise to two potential extremes: on one end, it is possible to commit tokens on aper
positionbasis, or as soon as a position has converged. It is also possible to commit tokensat a block
level, or after the entire denoising window has converged.
In this work, we use the Entropy Bound sampler used in DiffusionGemma (DiffusionGemma Team
et al., 2026) to study these extremes and uncover the underlying tradeoffs. Committing tokens on a
per position level removes the chance for later revision, which is particularly useful when trading off
performance for speed at higher entropy bounds. When accepted tokens are frozen, macro accuracy
falls from 73% to 20% as the entropy bound is relaxed, while the unfrozen sampler stays near 70%.
Committing on a block level results in many redundant computations, as tokens converge in a largely
left-to-right order. Thus a converged prefix sits idle in the window, and by twenty denoising steps
more than half of the slots, on average, are spent on tokens that are already final.
As either extreme results in losing sampler flexibility or tolerating wasted computation, we

Masked diffusion has seen success when scaling up diffusion language models (Nie et al., 2026; Ye

Block DiffusionBlock diffusion is a technique that interpolates between autoregressive order and
pure diffusion. Given a maximum context length𝐶, block diffusion splits this into evenly sized

diffusion requires recomputing attention across all positions every step.
2.2. Adaptive Samplers
Adaptive acceptance rules use model predictions to choose which positions to update. Prior work on
masked diffusion demonstrates the importance of generation order (Kim et al., 2025b). Towards this
end, training-free adaptive sampling algorithms are commonly used as they require no architectural
modifications (Nie et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025). In general, such algorithms typically rely on the
model’s logits to determine the ordering, although some also leverage the internal attention patterns
(Kim et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026).

3. Where and When: Two Choices for Every Position
In this section, we write existing samplers in terms of a routing rule and a commitment rule (Algo-
rithm 1, Table 1). We assume a diffusion model that learns the posterior𝑃(𝑋 0|𝑋𝑡), and takes both a
denoising window and a KV cache as input. The denoising window represents tokens that are actively
being denoised, and the KV cache corresponds to tokens that have been completely denoised.
At every time-step, the sampler must answer the following two questions:
1. Where does the token come from?Is it the predicted tokenˆ𝑋𝑖∼𝑃 𝜃(·|𝑋𝑡)𝑖, the previous value𝑋𝑖𝑡,
or a fresh noisy token drawn from𝜋?
2. When does the position get appended to the KV cache?Do we move the position to the KV cache,
or keep it in the denoising window?

rule takes the model’s prediction for the current step.

Adaptive Samplers as Routing RulesThe routing view was introduced to make this choice explicit:
Zheng et al. (2023) observe that the standard sampler routes every token with the same probability,
and that writing the router out allows more flexible decoding rules. Adaptive-order samplers fit
this view directly, as each one specifies the accept-stay-noise point for every position at every step
(Table 1). A related split appears in Liu et al. (2025), which separates planning (choosing which
positions to change) from denoising (predicting the clean token). Our addition is the second axis:
prior routing views describe where each token comes from, but not when a position is committed
to the KV cache. The shared notation allows acceptance rules to be compared across corruption
processes, while keeping their different noise distributions and constraints on future revisions explicit.

This leaves one hyper-parameter,𝛾, in control of both choices. Figure 1a shows that the unified rule

4. Per-Position Commitment: Losing the Chance for Revision
Committing tokens on a per-position basis removes the chance for later revision. We simulate this
consequence of per-position commitment by freezing each token as soon as it is accepted by the EB
rule. The main property we study is that the token can no longer change. We find that later revision
becomes increasingly useful when trading off performance for speed at higher entropy bounds, and
that self-correction persists without renoising.
SetupWe focus on the EB sampler, as it is the default sampler used in DiffusionGemma Team
et al. (2026). Our goal is to study the loss of sampler flexibility under per-position commitment,
and determine whether later revisions require explicit noise injection. To do this, we measure the
macro-average of LCB v6, Natural2Code, and GPQA Diamond. We vary two sampler choices: whether
accepted positions are frozen, and whether rejected positions are renoised. The model weights and
uniform corruption process are unchanged in all four conditions.
Frozen EB SamplerThe Frozen EB ablation simulates per-position commitment: once a position is
accepted under the EB rule, its token can no longer change. We retain the denoising window and
blockwise cache commitment to isolate the loss of later revision. This simulation does not reproduce
the attention changes or computational savings of actually moving individual positions into the KV
cache. We refer to the unmodified Unified EB sampler as “unconstrained”, as any position can be
changed regardless of whether it was already accepted under the EB rule.
No NoiseTo test whether revisions require explicit noise injection, we additionally disable renoising
of rejected positions. In this condition, rejected positions retain their current tokens, while accepted
predictions can still revise unfrozen positions. This isolates the role of explicit renoising without
changing the underlying model.
AblationRemoving noise and freezing accepted tokens are two independent interventions. Thus we
ablate over all four combinations and show the results in Figure 1b. For each possible combination,
we do a sweep over various entropy bound values to examine how they are affected by more relaxed

ResultsWe observe that for each adaptive variant, the unconstrained sampler achieves a superior

At the loosest bound, the frozen variants fall to about 20% macro Pass@1, while the unconstrained
variants stay near 70%.
Furthermore, the number of positions that change after initial acceptance increases with higher
entropy thresholds, and is relatively unaffected by whether the sampler uses noise injection. At
the default bound of0.1, roughly 97% to 99% of accepted positions never change, so the default
sampler behaves much like a frozen one; revisions become frequent only as the bound is relaxed. The
simulation shows the cost of removing later revision on a per-position basis: accepting a token once
does not mean that fixing it will preserve performance at higher entropy bounds. Self-correction also
persists without renoising, so explicit noise injection is not necessary for these revisions.
Per-position commitment therefore loses sampler flexibility as the entropy bound is relaxed. This
motivates waiting for more than an individual position to be accepted before removing its chance for
later revision.
5. Block-Level Commitment: Wasting Denoising Slots
At the other extreme, commitment is made at a block level: Unified EB retains every position in the
denoising window until the whole block satisfies its stopping rule. Here we examine the order in which
DiffusionGemma accepts tokens under the Unified EB sampler. We observe that DiffusionGemma
exhibits a sampling order strongly biased towards autoregressive generation. This autoregressive bias
results in many denoising slots within a canvas being "wasted" on tokens that have already converged.
As a result, a form of rolling sampler where the window moves incrementally through the sequence
rather than block by block becomes increasingly appealing as a way to extend the Pareto frontier.

computation on converged prefixes. Section 6 introduces RES to strike a balance: wait for a contiguous
accepted prefix, without waiting for the entire denoising window.
6. RES: A Rolling Sampler
Here we introduce the Rolling Entropy Sampler (RES) to strike a balance between per-position
commitment and block-level commitment. Rather than committing each token as soon as it is
accepted or waiting for the entire window, RES waits until a contiguous prefix of at least𝑠 positions
has been accepted by the EB rule. The core mechanism is simple: instead of waiting for the entire
block to converge, RES encodes any sufficiently long contiguous prefix of accepted tokens, appends it
to the KV cache, and refills the freed slots with fresh noise. An accepted position farther ahead remains
free to change until the preceding positions are also accepted and the minimum-shift condition is
met. Waiting for the contiguous prefix preserves the chance for later revision without retaining the
entire denoising window. Figure 4 illustrates the method.

non-trivial commitment rule: in addition to a routing rule𝐹𝑟𝑜𝑢𝑡𝑒 on the accept-stay-noise simplex, it
specifies𝐹 𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑖𝑡.
RES can be interpreted as an extension of the block EB sampler used in DiffusionGemma. Rather
than applying the entropy bound criterion solely to determine whether a prediction should be accepted
or not, we also use it to determine whether to commit a contiguous prefix to the KV cache. The
entropy bound criterion controls the predictive entropies of jointly accepted positions. Under the
low-prediction-error assumption discussed in Section 2, equation 6 approximately bounds the joint
dependence error incurred by factorized sampling (Ben-Hamu et al., 2026). Thus we use the EB
criterion to define𝐹𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑚𝑖𝑡 as well as𝐹𝑟𝑜𝑢𝑡𝑒: RES commits the longest prefix of the window whose

minimum shift requires acceptance across a sufficiently long prefix before commitment, while leaving
accepted positions farther ahead available for revision. When𝑠=𝑤 , the window size, we recover

window must be accepted before a prefix can be committed. Even at𝑠= 1, commitment requires
a prefix: an accepted position farther ahead cannot leave the window independently. Increasing𝑠
requires a longer accepted prefix and moves the rule toward block-level commitment.
6.1. Additional Modifications
RES includes two further design choices, and we also note a property that follows from them.
First, we use a position-based temperature schedule. Positions toward the left of the window have
10

been denoised for longer than those that have just entered on the right, and the schedule reflects this,
thus improving speed.
Second, we merge the encoding and denoising steps. RES commits short prefixes frequently, so a
separate encoding pass for each commit would cost more forward passes than rolling saves; merging
makes rolling commitment efficient. All reported TPF numbers count every forward pass for both
samplers, including Unified EB’s per-block encoding pass.
Finally, becausethetemperatureschedulenolongerdependsonthemaximumnumberofdenoising
steps, RES can be viewed as an anytime sampler.
Position-based Temperature ScheduleOne natural consequence of the autoregressive bias of
DiffusionGemma is that a token’s position in the rolling canvas provides a proxy for how close it is to
having converged. Thus we can modify the standard temperature shaping applied in DiffusionGemma
to be based on position rather than noise level. This accelerates tokens that are close to the prefix, as
these are closer to being committed, while allowing for positions in the suffix to explore candidate
tokens.
Simultaneous Encoding and DenoisingOne other advantage of RES is that the number of tokens
appended to the KV cache is much smaller than the total canvas. This allows for merging both the
encoding step and the denoising step present in DiffusionGemma. More specifically, given a window
of𝑤 positions and a contiguous prefix ofℓ accepted tokens, we use a causal mask for positions0to
ℓ− 1, while positionsℓ to𝑤− 1have bidirectional attention. This keeps the number of processed
positions per forward pass the same between the original DiffusionGemma sampler and RES, while
ensuring that every step is performing denoising. We include a visualization of how the attention
mask changes alongside the steps of RES in Figure 4.
Anytime SamplingWhile Unified EB is highly adaptive, the temperature schedule is tied to the
maximum DNS, which is a hyper-parameter that must be set. Ideally, the maximum DNS would only
serve a time-out function to prevent sampler stalls, as opposed to controlling the logit shaping. One
consequence of adopting the position-based temperature schedule is that the logit shaping becomes
independent of the maximum number of DNS, making it ananytime sampler: the maximum number
of denoising steps serves as a budget limit rather than controlling fundamental sampler behavior.

7.3. Multi-Sample Performance
RES also scales better with the number of samples (Figure 5). Unified EB has the higher Pass@1, but
RES with𝑠= 32overtakes it from𝑘≥ 6samples and maintains its advantage through𝑘= 20. We
hypothesize that the EB sampler avoids making “difficult” decisions, as it always accepts the lowest
entropy positions first. In contrast, the rolling mechanism along with the position-based temperature
schedule enforces a left-to-right generation bias, which was shown in Ni et al. (2026) to improve
diversity across generations.
8. Related Works
Here we discuss works that are closely related to the framework and sampler modifications proposed
in this work.
8.1. Adaptive Block Samplers
Our work is not the first to suggest incrementally moving the denoising window: Luo et al. (2026)
applies this to masked diffusion and sees performance gains. Gat et al. (2025) fine-tunes an auto-
13
RES: Accelerating Diffusion Language Models with Rolling Entropy Sampling2026-11-20
regressive model to simultaneously decode the masked tokens ahead using the EB sampler of Ben-
Hamu et al. (2026) while generating the next token auto-regressively, which is the same core idea of
a rolling denoising window. Furthermore, the denoising window is entirely defined by the entropy
bound, which has been already defined in Ben-Hamu et al. (2026) and used in the original sampler
of DiffusionGemma Team et al. (2026).
Similar ideas of combining diffusion with the left-to-right bias of language have appeared even
earlier, such as in (Wu et al., 2023). Our contribution is a study of the tradeoff between per-position
commitment and block-level commitment, using DiffusionGemma as the experimental setting. Frozen
EB simulates the loss of later revision under per-position commitment. RES waits for a contiguous
accepted prefix, preserving sampler flexibility for accepted positions farther ahead while avoiding
redundant computation on the prefix. The monotone masked-diffusion rules in Table 1 provide related
examples of preventing later revision on a per-position basis, though freezing does not necessarily
imply immediate KV caching.
8.2. Rolling at Training Time: Clock Diffusion
Concurrent with our work, Schiff et al. (2026) introduce Clock Diffusion, which develops essentially
the same idea for Gaussian diffusion: a window that rolls left to right and commits finished positions to
the KV cache, with a position-dependent noise schedule that mirrors our position-based temperature
schedule.
8.3. Rolling Diffusion Beyond Text
Beyond text generation, the idea of rolling diffusion has been studied extensively in other domains.
Within video generation, rolling diffusion was a natural mechanism to address the temporal nature of
video generation and avoid performing diffusion on every frame simultaneously (Ruhe et al., 2024).
Many other works include similar mechanisms (Chen et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025),
and Papa et al. (2026) carry the idea over to images by assigning different patches different levels of
corruption.
9. Conclusion
We studied two choices made by diffusion language samplers: where the next token comes from,
and when the position is committed to the KV cache. Using the EB sampler in DiffusionGemma, we
examined per-position commitment and block-level commitment. Freezing tokens upon acceptance
simulates the loss of later revision under per-position commitment, which degrades performance at
higher entropy bounds. Block-level commitment instead tolerates redundant computation on positions
that have already converged. RES strikes a balance by waiting for a sufficiently long contiguous
accepted prefix. Accepted positions farther ahead retain the chance for revision, while the prefix
can leave without waiting for the entire window. Across seven benchmarks, RES raises tokens per
forward pass by 32.5% at an average cost of 1.9 points of accuracy and extends the frontier at high
throughput. Instruction following degrades more than other tasks, and the block sampler remains
better at low throughput.
